# The Adventures of Little Moon and the Star Garden
## RPG concept and production brief — v1

Date: November 13, 2026, America/Chicago  
Status: Proposed design synthesis. The two supplied concept images were reviewed. Earlier project decisions were recovered from conversation context. No game implementation, device playtest, new learning-outcome study, or audit of the previous code packages was performed for this brief.

## 1. The game in one paragraph

A family storybook RPG in which Rosalie and Little Moon explore a luminous archipelago, recruit animal companions, and restore the paths between gardens and stars. Players solve environmental problems by combining a small set of dependable abilities: grow useful magical plants, ask companions to carry out visible plans, and leave moonlit echoes of actions they actually performed. Major chapters introduce capabilities; smaller adventures explore new combinations and add lasting changes to the world. The garden becomes a place to inhabit, a record of shared adventures, and a useful part of the next journey.

**Player promise: A little adventure. A world that remembers.**

The experience should be understandable in a shared scene with a very young child and strategically interesting when played with fuller controls. These are selectable participation modes, not developmental grades.

## 3. The story: the map that no longer fits

Every constellation has a resting place in the Star Garden. Tiny traveling stars sleep in lanternflowers, then follow luminous paths back into the sky.

One evening, a playful star shower tangles those paths. Lights arrive in unexpected places. One ends up in Dunz's delivery basket. Another wakes a hillside of musical mushrooms. A third insists it is a firefly.

Little Moon arrives with a map that no longer matches the world.

Rosalie discovers that she can reconnect the islands by understanding their inhabitants and making things that belong there. She and Little Moon set out to prepare a new route for the Star Parade.

The immediate stakes are understandable: friends cannot visit, a nursery lacks water, a ferry has lost its song, and a celebration needs a way through the islands. The parade waits for the player. There is no real-world deadline.

The larger mystery is why the old map keeps failing. Eventually the player discovers that the world has grown. The final route must incorporate the bridges, gardens, shelters, and musical paths the player has made.

The story therefore supports persistent consequences without treating the child as responsible for preventing catastrophe. It has uncertainty, setbacks, surprises, and work worth doing.

### Opening scene

Dunz delivers an enormous basket labeled:

FRAGILE. PROBABLY A TURNIP.

Something inside sneezes a star.

Rosalie opens it. Little Moon floats out upside down, holding his map.

Little Moon: “Well. This is not the observatory.”

Solara: “It might still be the right place.”

Little Moon: “That sounds suspiciously like another adventure.”

The player makes a flower respond before encountering a settings screen or a long explanation. Within the first scene, the player notices a problem, chooses an action, and changes the world.

## 4. Character relationships

Rosalie is the story's explorer and decision-maker. She chooses destinations, experiments, asks for help, and decides where things belong. A future public edition can offer an original customizable explorer alongside the story heroine; personal photographs are not needed for the core game.

Little Moon is a travel partner with useful magic and imperfect knowledge. He reveals details, preserves actions as Moonprints, and helps the player interpret what happened. He also needs help, changes his mind, and occasionally has a ridiculous plan.

His humor aims at weather, maps, gardening inconveniences, and his own assumptions. It never humiliates the player.

Examples:

- After an unexpected plant grows: “I asked for a flower. We appear to have ordered a staircase.”
- When a plan does not work: “The bridge has offered some feedback.”
- After the player finds another route: “That was not on my map. I shall blame the map.”
- At a stopping point: “A respectable amount of adventure. Shall we put the stars to bed?”

Solara can appear at chapter openings, the home garden, and endings as the garden keeper and storyteller. She invites possibilities; Little Moon notices what a possibility will require. Their disagreements create warmth and humor without turning every interaction into a lecture.

### Companion ensemble

| Companion | Character texture | Useful ability | A meaningful limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| TonGee | Enthusiastic explorer who sometimes volunteers before looking | Scouts routes, leaps to marked ledges, tests sturdy surfaces | Cannot carry heavy building materials |
| Mono | Playful improviser who treats ordinary objects as instruments | Reaches canopy anchors, swings between points, connects vines | Needs a reachable anchor and clear space |
| Deddy | Gentle builder with large hands and careful movements | Braces structures, moves heavy objects, steadies platforms | Cannot enter narrow tunnels |
| Momo | Affectionate tracker easily distracted by interesting smells | Follows scent trails, retrieves lightweight objects | Needs a trail or a visible target |
| Batman | Quiet observer who notices something before deciding to mention it | Reveals hidden movement, small passages, and overlooked clues | Offers hints rather than completing the player's decision |
| Lily | Practical gardener with opinions about where things should grow | Tends plants, redirects water, identifies habitat needs | Plants still need suitable conditions |
| Dunz | Reliable traveler whose ordinary deliveries become adventures | Carries supplies, remembers routes, transports bulky discoveries | Follows navigable ground paths |
| Tia | Monarch traveler who sees the landscape from a different angle | Reveals wind currents, pollinates suitable plants, scouts overhead | Cannot move heavy items or act as a universal flying platform |

Each companion also needs a personal project and a small story arc. Abilities give them gameplay purpose; preferences, mistakes, jokes, and relationships give them character.

No companion represents a score for kindness, bravery, patience, or emotional maturity.

## 5. The connected play loop

The repeatable loop is:

**Notice something interesting → discover what it needs → choose a plan → act with friends and magic → see the consequence → bring something useful home.**

A chapter should offer a clear central intention, optional discoveries, and room to experiment. The home garden should be useful between chapters: try a new seed, watch a companion use a structure, rearrange a path, or revisit a memory.

Progression adds verbs and relationships:

1. Reveal a hidden path.
2. Grow a useful plant.
3. Remember an action.
4. Coordinate a friend.
5. Combine two abilities.
6. Change a local environmental condition.
7. Design a route that other characters can use.

Optional titles such as Seed Finder, Path Maker, and Star Keeper can mark story milestones. They should not imply a rating of the child.

## 6. Signature mechanic one: Moonprints

Little Moon can preserve a short action as a glowing echo.

### The first encounter

The explorer stands on a moon-sensitive petal pad. Its light opens a water channel. Little Moon offers “Remember this.” A translucent echo remains on the pad while the explorer walks away.

The player sees the causal chain: light on pad, channel open, water flowing. The echo holds its endpoint until dismissed. There is no race against a countdown.

### The fuller ability

Later, the player can record a short route and supported light actions, see the path as a ribbon, then start its replay. The echo follows the recorded movement and holds its final pose. It might keep a lantern aimed at a moonflower or repeat a light pattern while the player works elsewhere.

The recommended initial maximum is one active Moonprint. A second belongs to a later optional challenge tier, after one print is understandable.

### Rule contract

- A Moonprint replays recorded movement and explicitly supported actions.
- Moonprints activate moon-responsive pads, flowers, and light relays.
- They do not duplicate inventory, seeds, rewards, or physical quest objects.
- They cannot lift an ordinary heavy log merely because the original character did.
- An obstruction introduced after recording stops the print visibly.
- Recording or replacing a print is free.
- Replay can be stopped, inspected, and restarted.
- The world does not rewind.
- Every persistence boundary must define whether the print resumes or returns to a clear ready state.
- A simplified mode can use an authored demonstration or a single remembered position.

The source idea comes from IN-TIME's described recorded echoes. The light-sensitive interaction rules are a deliberate new adaptation for this game, not a claim that the existing engine already implements them.

Moonprints should be one tool among several. Some quests should be solvable without them so the campaign does not become a sequence of repeated switch puzzles.

## 7. Signature mechanic two: Living Seeds

Plants are functional objects with readable behavior. The player learns a small family of rules and combines them across the world.

| Plant | Main behavior | Conditions | Example use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lanternflower | Holds and redirects gentle light | Suitable soil and an available light source | Reveal a route or signal a creature |
| Ribbonvine | Connects nearby marked anchors | Two reachable supports | Create a crossing or handhold |
| Sailleaf | Opens into a buoyant leaf or catches wind | Water or a visible breeze, depending on variety | Ferry a small passenger or move a light platform |
| Bellbud | Produces a repeating musical and visual pattern | A chosen pattern and compatible receiver | Call a ferry or coordinate a sequence |

Fantasy rules should be consistent. Real botanical or animal information, when included, should be separately checked and should not be inferred from magical plant behavior.

The basic crafting grammar is simple: choose a seed, place it where its conditions are met, and provide the missing support. Deeper chapters ask players to combine those plants with a companion or a Moonprint.

Inventory should appear when choosing or crafting. The normal play screen does not need permanent star, leaf, and heart counters.

Seeds are reusable capabilities. Experimentation should not depend on a long resource grind, paid energy, or waiting until tomorrow. Undoing a placement returns the usable seed or materials.

### The garden is part of progression

A recovered seed gives both a home feature and a new exploration possibility.

- A lantern pond lights a home path and provides Sailleaf practice.
- A vine arbor creates a climbing nook and unlocks a new anchor connection.
- A bell-flower patch adds a musical layer to the garden and supplies a signal for later journeys.

The garden's changes should be causally connected to adventures. A random decoration reward provides less meaning than a plant whose purpose the player already understands.

## 8. Signature mechanic three: Companion Plans

Characters should act intelligently within the world without taking away the player's choices.

Basic interaction: select a friend, then select a valid object. Lily waters the chosen plant. Mono reaches the chosen anchor. Momo follows the chosen trail.

Advanced interaction: arrange a short illustrated plan, for example:

**Go to the marked tree → attach the vine → wait.**

The player sees the intended route and target before execution. A companion can pause and explain a missing requirement. It must not pretend success.

Companion autonomy includes following, avoiding obstacles, waiting for the player, noticing relevant clues, and proposing help. The player controls consequential choices such as which route to build or where to plant a reward.

A later tactical setup can use two selected companions, two seed abilities, and one Moonprint. This is a proposed starting constraint to test, not a fixed balance claim. The initial tutorial should expose only one relevant action at a time.

Companion selection changes available routes. An easy return or swap point prevents a poor choice from stranding the player.

This provides strategy through capabilities, position, sequence, and conditions. It does not require damage numbers or a combat system.

## 9. Six worlds with distinct rules and stories

The region names come from the supplied images. The quests and mechanics below are new proposals.

| Region | Adventure identity | Main interaction | Lasting change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Color Cove | A harbor of pigment springs, reflective shells, and colored light | Redirect light, combine pigments, mark a useful route; symbols also identify colors | The harbor and home lanterns carry the player's chosen palette |
| Animal Island | Canopy routes, hidden tracks, nests, and visiting creatures | Follow tracks, combine companion abilities, build an appropriate crossing or habitat | New creatures use the route and visit the garden |
| Shape Summit | A mountain workshop of rotating bridges and movable houses | Place, balance, brace, and connect structures | A player-designed structure becomes part of the world |
| Nature Nook | Streams, root tunnels, nursery beds, and weather pockets | Redirect water and grow useful plants while keeping routes open | Restored water reaches a nursery and the home garden |
| Rhythm Reef | A coastal orchestra whose melodies move ferries and wake shell gates | Arrange patterns with sound, animation, and visible symbols | The recovered melody joins the garden soundtrack |
| Feeling Forest | A lantern festival where different friends want different kinds of participation | Listen to what a character says, notice clues, offer a suitable plan | The celebration includes quiet, active, solo, and shared spaces |

Feeling Forest is not an emotion-recognition test. A character can say “I want to watch first,” and the player can create a viewing spot. The game should not decide that a child is sad or anxious from their face, voice, or play behavior.

A later world may introduce a visible breeze, dewfall, or local moonlight condition. These replace opaque world modifiers with understandable environmental rules. They are discovered or chosen inside the adventure; they do not force the family to play at a real-world time.

### Challenge and spectacle

The game needs memorable set pieces. A giant garden creature can block a route, a playful wind can move platforms, or a traveling Moonwhale can need a lantern path through hanging gardens.

Such encounters can have stages, coordinated abilities, surprising behavior, and satisfying resolution. Precision timing is optional or adjustable. Failure creates clear feedback and a nearby retry while preserving discoveries.

These encounters are later campaign proposals. They are outside the first playable scope.

## 10. A complete first adventure

### The Lantern That Couldn't Get Home

Target: one compact adventure with a garden hub, a stream, a nursery, and a canopy route. A full playthrough might take roughly 10–20 minutes, but that is a design target to measure. A family can stop and resume at each meaningful step.

Cast in the playable scope: Rosalie, Little Moon, Lily, and Mono. Other characters can be present in static portraits or later content, but the slice does not require their full animation and AI.

Initial control scope: Together mode with optional tap navigation, replayable narration and captions, a one-step hint button, select-and-place alternatives to dragging, pause, and reduced motion. The full Storytime presentation, advanced Adventure plans, and simultaneous cooperative controls are subsequent increments.

Initial Moonprint scope: remember one actual illuminated position and hold it. Recorded movement paths and multi-action replay belong to a later increment. Both routes below prove the simplest mechanic without requiring that later functionality.

Goal: help a small sleeping star reach its lanternflower on the far bank.

The bridge is broken. Lily notices a curled ferry leaf beside a dry basin. Mono points out anchors above the stream. Both possibilities are visible before the player chooses.

### Common opening

1. Walk or tap toward the star.
2. See it respond to Little Moon's light.
3. Reach the stream and inspect the broken crossing.
4. Meet Lily and Mono.
5. Choose a route using two large illustrated possibilities.

There is a recoverable way back from either choice. The other route remains discoverable.

### Route A: grow a ferry

1. Trace the dry channel from the basin to a moon-sensitive water gate.
2. Stand on the petal pad and see the gate open.
3. Ask Little Moon to remember that position.
4. Leave the Moonprint supplying light while Lily helps tend the leaf.
5. Place a Sailleaf seed in the prepared basin.
6. Watch it unfold into a ferry.
7. Bring the star aboard and follow a broad, readable route across the stream.

The player controls cause and sequence. Assistance can handle steering. A bump into the bank creates a mild reaction and a chance to adjust, not lost progress.

### Route B: connect the canopy

1. Notice a near anchor and the outline of a far anchor behind a folded moonflower.
2. Supply light to open the flower.
3. Leave a Moonprint maintaining that light.
4. Ask Mono to reach the revealed anchor.
5. Connect a Ribbonvine between the valid supports using a snapping placement interaction.
6. Cross the completed route with the star.

The player solves a spatial support problem. Mono's ability matters, and the visible light condition explains why the anchor is available.

### Safe completion and release

Moonlight enables preparation. Once the ferry has fully grown, it remains afloat when the water gate closes. Once both vine anchors are secured, the crossing remains usable and the route stays clear when the Moonprint is dismissed. Completed construction never depends on leaving the echo active forever.

Before completion, releasing light pauses the local preparation and allows a free restart. Any interrupted traversal returns the explorer and star to the nearest valid safe point without duplicating objects or removing discoveries. The puzzle must remain solvable after cancellation, departure, and reload.

### Resolution

The star settles into its lanternflower. The new crossing remains in the world. A friend uses it during the return, proving that the construction matters beyond the puzzle.

At home, the player chooses where to plant a related Star Seed. The ferry route can inspire a small lantern pond; the canopy route can inspire a vine nook. Both can be acquired later. The first choice shapes the current garden without locking out the other forever.

The game records an actual event such as: “Rosalie and Lily grew the ferry.”

Little Moon: “A boat made of a leaf. I shall be reviewing the rest of my assumptions.”

The scene closes with a short musical phrase and a clear resting place. The player can continue, rearrange the garden, or stop.

## 11. Family participation and deeper play

| Mode | What the player controls | Complexity | Suitable purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Storytime | Touching characters, making one visible choice, triggering an environmental response | Adult handles navigation and sequence | Shared discovery with a very young child or someone who wants a guided story |
| Together | Exploration, contextual actions, a companion choice, garden placement | Navigation help and stepwise hints available | Family cooperation and early independent experiments |
| Adventure | Movement, route choice, companion plans, seed combinations, optional discoveries | Adjustable puzzle support and fuller controls | Players who want the complete RPG |

These are full-game participation modes, not locked age bands. A player can change mode without losing the garden or receiving a lesser ending. The first slice implements the Together controls specified above; the other presentations require their own implementation and verification.

For Rosalie now, the proposed entry is a brief shared scene: you guide the adventure, she touches a character, chooses a flower, points to a route, or enjoys the response. The richer planning systems can be available as her interests and participation change. This is a product hypothesis, not a developmental assessment or recommendation about screen duration.

### Cooperative roles

In an expanded version, one player controls the explorer and another controls Little Moon.

The explorer selects destinations, requests companion help, and places garden features. Little Moon illuminates, steadies a crossing, reveals a clue, and helps with recording.

For the first prototype, same-device shared play and assisted solo behavior are sufficient. Simultaneous two-controller play is a separate implementation gate; network multiplayer is not needed to prove the concept.

### Learning through situations

A child can encounter shape, pattern, language, cause and effect, and different perspectives while pursuing a meaningful adventure. Examples include choosing a shape that braces a bridge, noticing a creature's track, or arranging a repeating reef pattern.

NAEYC's developmentally appropriate practice guidance emphasizes child choice and guided play, with adults supporting an environment while children retain agency. That informs this direction; it does not establish learning outcomes for this untested game. [S1]

## 12. The garden as a living record

The garden should show what has happened through behavior and use.

A repaired path becomes a companion's shortcut. The player sees Tia visit a flowering plant. Mono plays the tune recovered at the reef. A lantern reveals a previously dark corner.

The memory book can replay short in-game moments or show an illustrated summary from verified events. It must distinguish game history from family memories.

Examples:

- Game event: “You and Mono connected the canopy path.”
- Caregiver-authored note: “Rosalie pointed at the butterfly today.”
- Unacceptable automatic invention: “Rosalie overcame her fear and felt proud.”

Decorations can be changed. Rebuilding should update the world while preserving the factual record that an earlier version existed. Persistent history should not mean permanent regret.

### The most personal later feature: children's artwork in the world

A caregiver could choose a drawing and place it on a kite, garden flag, sign, constellation panel, or flower pattern.

Preserve the original marks. The child should recognize the drawing. Offer cropping or simple placement, not automatic replacement with polished generic artwork.

A bounded recipe can determine behavior separately: choose whether a drawing decorates a lantern, a flag, or a leaf. Image interpretation should not invent unpredictable game physics.

This feature is not part of the initial gameplay proof. It is a strong later expression of the world remembering its players.

### Beyond the screen

At a natural stop, a companion can offer an optional activity: make a leaf boat with a grown-up, draw a garden creature, listen for a bird, or find shapes in everyday objects.

The family can skip it without losing game progress. Photographic proof and automatic surveillance are unnecessary.

## 13. AI that serves the adventure

The useful ambition is responsive characters and coherent world memory.

### Initial implementation

Use authored dialogue, state machines or small planners, navigation, and recorded events. These can support companions noticing a clue, offering a plan, waiting, and remembering a completed quest. The core experience works offline.

The player sees behavior, not a label claiming that every character is an autonomous intelligence.

### Optional later generation

A model can assist adult-facing story creation or propose variations from an approved collection of characters, actions, locations, and vocabulary. Generated suggestions must be validated before they enter the playable world.

The game simulation owns inventory, positions, quest status, unlocks, and save data. A model cannot declare a task complete or invent a past event.

A sensible pipeline is:

**Available world facts → proposed allowed action → rule validation → visible action → recorded result.**

If a service is unavailable, the adventure continues with authored content.

Character dialogue should retain canonical voice. Little Moon should not become an endlessly agreeable tutor. He can make a gentle observation, ask one useful question, or admit an uncertainty.

The core design does not need unrestricted private chat, emotional dependency language, child profiling, facial emotion inference, or an always-on microphone. Family personalization can begin with selected names, colors, language, and avatar choices.

UNICEF's current guidance emphasizes children's safety, privacy, inclusion, and understandable explanations of AI. These principles support the constrained design above; they are not evidence that any proposed implementation is safe or compliant. [S2]

## 14. Visual direction, sound, and interface

### Visual target

A playable, handcrafted storybook diorama with tangible materials:

- Deep blue night skies and warm amber treehouses.
- Soft leaf textures, brushed wood, recognizable fur, and expressive eyes.
- Moonlight used to reveal routes and interaction states.
- Restrained particles that direct attention.
- A fixed or gently following camera with clear foreground cutaways.
- Distinct silhouettes for Rosalie, Little Moon, companions, and echoes.

The reference images are rich promotional compositions. The game screen should reveal that richness through exploration. It should present one immediate intention, a readable environment, and a small set of relevant actions.

### Main screen

The home screen is the garden. A friend offers an adventure. A storybook opens the map. A lantern offers pause or rest.

During play, the interface might show the relevant action, current companion, and one Moonprint control. More controls appear when the player needs them.

The adult area contains settings, language, assistance, memory editing, exports, and caregiver material. It need not expose household classifications in the child's adventure.

The “no pressure, no scores” promise should be reflected in the interface. Permanent currency counters and repeated grading screens would create a conflicting experience. Material quantities can still be shown locally where a construction decision requires them.

### Sound and language

Use memorable short melodies, environmental reactions, and character-specific musical textures. Completed adventures can add layers to the garden's music.

English and Spanish should be full language options, with optional bilingual phrase support. Voice, captions, and replay should align. A child should not need microphone input to progress.

### Accessibility

Use redundant cues: color plus shape or symbol, sound plus animation, speech plus captions, and drag actions with select-and-place alternatives.

Let players adjust navigation help, timing assistance, and puzzle hints independently. Keep reading and choice interfaces available until dismissed. Reduced-motion and reduced-effects options should preserve information.

Microsoft's game accessibility guidance explicitly distinguishes different forms of difficulty and recommends configurable support. Its time-limit guidance addresses adequate time for interface interactions; it should not be misrepresented as banning all timed game mechanics. These are useful implementation references, not completed accessibility certification. [S3, S4]

## 15. Architecture and reuse

Audit the Garden Moon prototype and the IN-TIME package independently before selecting modules.

| Candidate source | Possible reuse | Required verification |
|---|---|---|
| Garden Moon / Play Quest | Character names, world content, caregiver activities, existing interaction patterns | Determine actual source authority, completeness, licensing, and current runtime behavior |
| IN-TIME | Deterministic events, recorded movement, navigation, input abstraction, save portability | Inspect code boundaries, test behavior, and verify suitability for new magical interaction rules |
| New game code | Companion intentions, seed rules, garden construction, assisted interactions, canonical character performance | Implement and verify as new work |
| New art and sound | Character models, environment kit, animations, musical layers | Confirm consistency, readability, performance, and approvals |

Keep a deterministic game simulation separate from presentation. Movement, object states, companion actions, and plant conditions should produce a clear event record. Dialogue and the memory book read that record.

Prefer a small number of reusable world components: light receivers, water gates, anchors, plant sockets, traversal routes, companion action targets, and persistent construction objects.

A scene should communicate which objects accept which actions. Internal data should distinguish reversible puzzle state from durable discoveries and completed constructions.

For a browser-first build, verify the actual target tablet/browser and rendering path. The earlier environment's reported fallback-renderer tests do not establish this game's native GPU performance, touch behavior, or normal-origin saves.

This is not a recommendation to replace the existing code with a new framework before auditing it. The first production objective is the smallest complete adventure with a dependable save path.

## 16. Production sequence

### Increment A: interaction proof

Build a graybox garden and stream. Verify movement or tap navigation, one light-sensitive pad, one Moonprint, one plant reaction, and one companion action. The player must see why each event occurs.

### Increment B: complete story slice

Add the two quest routes, return home, persistent garden placement, authored dialogue, and reliable pause/resume. Include a beginning, change, resolution, and stopping point.

### Increment C: presentation and input

Apply the intended character style, readable animations, restrained lighting, music, English/Spanish content structure, touch controls, captions, and assistance options. Validate on the selected physical device and browser.

### Increment D: family playtest

Observe families with consent. Check comprehension, control, delight, confusion, adult intervention, and stopping. Do not infer general learning benefits from engagement or successful task completion.

### Increment E: world expansion

Add a second region only after the first supports understandable experimentation. Expand the same verbs before inventing a separate minigame system for every island.

Network multiplayer, a large seamless world, a live generative child-chat layer, an in-game marketplace, and automatic photo/voice ingestion are outside the first product proof. They are not needed to test the central game.

## 17. Acceptance criteria for the first slice

These are proposed future gates, not passed tests.

| Gate | Observable evidence |
|---|---|
| Immediate agency | A new player can trigger a meaningful world reaction early, without a long explanation |
| Quest readability | The lost star, blocked crossing, and available possibilities are legible through scene design and optional narration |
| Causal Moonprint | The print holds a position where the player actually supplied light; its world effect is visible, with no hidden quest-completion shortcut; recorded movement is a later gate |
| Two valid routes | Ferry and canopy routes can each be completed through ordinary controls |
| Companion reliability | Friends carry out valid requests, signal blocked requests, and never silently substitute success |
| Persistent consequence | The completed route and chosen home feature survive a normal save/reload |
| Recovery | Pause, interruption, failed placement, echo dismissal, and invalid save data preserve or recover to a clear valid state; completed crossings stay usable |
| Input | Touch is directly exercised; any controller or cooperative claim is tested separately |
| Accessibility | Critical information has more than one channel; timing, navigation, and reading barriers can be reduced |
| Language | Core prompts and controls remain understandable in supported language modes |
| Offline behavior | The complete authored adventure works without a model service or account |
| Ending | The quest resolves, the garden acknowledges what happened, and stopping feels complete |

A useful playtest question is: Can the player show or explain what made the crossing work?

Other measures include how often an adult has to take control, whether a player notices the lasting change on return, whether assistance is easy to find, and whether a new attempt feels inviting. Session length and repeat visits should not be treated as the sole indicators of success.

## 18. Design review receipt

Independent creative input was obtained for narrative, mechanics, and product feasibility. The integrated design was reviewed through interaction, information architecture, visual systems, accessibility, platform, quality, research/trust, AI workflow, and content lenses. This is expert ideation, not empirical user research.

### Two viable formats

Scores are qualitative design estimates on a 1–5 scale; they are not measured results. Higher impact and reversibility are favorable. Higher effort and risk indicate a greater production burden.

| Option | Expected impact | Effort | Reversibility | Risk | Current evidence |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Guided illustrated scenes with branching interactions | 3 | 2 | 5 | 2 | Fits shared story interaction; limited evidence for deeper RPG play |
| Connected fixed-camera diorama RPG with shared-story assistance | 5 | 4 | 4 | 3 | Best fit for persistent construction and companion plans; requires a real slice and device testing |

Recommendation: use the connected diorama as the adventure foundation and offer Storytime through assistance and scene presentation. Avoid promising equal interaction demands across all participants.

### Material tensions and resolutions

- Echoes could overwhelm the simplest play mode. Introduce a remembered position first; reserve recorded routes for fuller controls.
- Companion automation could remove agency. Let companions offer and execute bounded help while the player chooses consequential plans.
- The large cast could overwhelm production. Fully implement only Lily and Mono alongside Rosalie and Little Moon in the slice.
- The garden could become a maintenance obligation. Preserve it during absence and make revisiting welcoming.
- Personalized memory could become fabricated biography. Separate recorded game facts from caregiver-authored real-life notes.
- The images' rich dashboard could obscure play. Use the world as the primary interface and disclose tools contextually.
- The older furry-mascot direction and current orb artwork differ. Preserve the current image reference and explore a tactile luminous model without claiming a canon change.
- A broad six-world vision could hide a weak central mechanic. Require one satisfying, complete adventure before expansion.

Accessibility, save integrity, or misleading memory behavior remain reasons to revise the design, regardless of visual appeal.

## 19. The finale

The final Star Parade uses the player's actual creations.

The lanterns wear the colors chosen in Color Cove. Friends arrive over the paths and crossings the player built. The reef melody joins Mono's instruments. Quiet spaces and lively spaces both fill with guests. The garden's stars rise into a constellation shaped by the journey.

A final puzzle combines familiar abilities in a new arrangement. The player directs the plan; companions and Moonprints make it possible.

Different routes produce different celebrations. They are complete endings, without ranking one child as better than another.

Little Moon studies the new constellation.

“That was not on my map.”

Solara smiles.

“It is now.”

The lasting product identity is a world where the player's actions become useful memories, and those memories make room for the next adventure.
